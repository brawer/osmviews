\section{Ranking places}\label{sec:ranking}
Many uses of OSMViews come down to putting things that have a location in
order: which shops to check first, which trees to map, which edit proposals
to review. We therefore treat OSMViews as a \emph{ranking function}, which
maps a longitude and latitude to a score, and compare it with other ranking
functions on \emph{sample sets}, the points that get ranked. Keeping the two
apart matters, because how much two functions agree depends on which points
they rank: random points on land mostly ask whether a function tells towns
from wilderness, while the branches of a chain store all lie in towns, and
ask whether it tells busy streets from quiet ones.

\subsection{Ranking functions}
Each function is read at its own resolution, at the cell that contains the
point, so the comparison is with what a user of each dataset would get
(Table~\ref{tab:functions}). Gridded data is turned into a density per
km\textsuperscript{2}; point data is counted per Web Mercator pixel, with
65{,}536 pixels across the world (about 600\,m at the equator), and
divided by the pixel's area. When a point is scored by the density of its
own source, such as a Wikidata item by the density of Wikidata items, it is
left out of its own count. A uniformly random score serves as a baseline.

\subsection{Sample sets}
From each source we draw \resRankingLandPoints{} points at random with a
fixed seed (Table~\ref{tab:samples}). Random land is spread evenly by area
over land outside Antarctica~\cite{naturalearth}; random people is drawn in
proportion to GHS-POP population~\cite{ghsl}. AllThePlaces~\cite{atp}
collects points from the websites of companies and public bodies; we split
its spiders by their declared lineage into brands (chain stores and other
branded places), infrastructure (mostly municipal GIS data such as street
lamps, benches and hydrants) and governments (public offices), and put
trees from municipal tree inventories in a group of their own. Overture
places are those with a confidence of at least 0.5.

\subsection{Measures}
A function can only rank points it scores differently, so for every
function and sample set we report the share of points that are
\emph{tied}, scored the same as some other point (usually zero). Agreement
between two functions is Kendall's $\tau_b$, which accounts for
ties~\cite{kendall}; it is the probability that the two put a random pair
of points in the same order, minus the probability that they put it in the
opposite order. Spearman's rank correlation and the overlap of the top
1\,\% are in the generated results. For the skew of a function, we fit
$\text{score} \propto \text{rank}^{-s}$ over the positive scores, from rank
10 to the 90th percentile of ranks; $s$ near 1 is Zipf's law.

\subsection{How the functions behave}
OSMViews gives a score above zero to almost every point of every sample
set except random land (Table~\ref{tab:ties}). On random land it scores
\resRankingLandOsmviewsZeroPct\,\% of points zero, against
\resRankingLandPopulationZeroPct\,\% for population and
\resRankingLandLightsZeroPct\,\% for night lights. On places of interest the
difference is larger still: the densities of photos and Wikidata items,
leave \resRankingBrandsPhotosTiedPct\,\% and
\resRankingBrandsItemsTiedPct\,\% of ATP brands tied, mostly at zero,
while OSMViews leaves \resRankingBrandsOsmviewsTiedPct\,\% tied. Most of these ties are points on the same zoom-18 pixel. Tree
inventories and municipal infrastructure are so dense that many of their
points share a pixel, which is why OSMViews ties
\resRankingTreesOsmviewsTiedPct\,\% of ATP trees; a finer ranking within
a street needs a finer function. Population and night lights are smooth,
so they rarely tie in towns, but they also cannot tell a shopping street
from the residential block next to it.

OSMViews is also among the most skewed functions. Fitted over the ATP
brands, its scores fall off as $\text{rank}^{-s}$ with
$s = \resRankingBrandsOsmviewsZipf$, close to the density of photos
(\resRankingBrandsPhotosZipf) and steeper than population
(\resRankingBrandsPopulationZipf) or the density of places
(\resRankingBrandsPlacesZipf): a few places are viewed far more than the
rest.

\subsection{How much the functions agree}
Agreement with OSMViews ranges from $\tau_b$ around 0.1 to 0.7
(Table~\ref{tab:agreement}); the random baseline is zero throughout. It is
lowest on random land, where most points are wilderness that every
function scores zero or nearly so, and higher on every sample set of
things people want to rank. The highest value is between OSMViews and
night lights on ATP infrastructure
(\resRankingInfrastructureLightsKendallOsmviews), plausibly because much of
that set is street lamps. On ATP brands, population agrees best
(\resRankingBrandsPopulationKendallOsmviews).

These values are moderate, but the other functions agree no better with
each other (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). On ATP brands, the mean $\tau_b$ of
OSMViews with the other six functions is
\resRankingBrandsOsmviewsKendallMean, against
\resRankingBrandsPopulationKendallMean{} for population,
\resRankingBrandsLightsKendallMean{} for night lights and
\resRankingBrandsPlacesKendallMean{} for places; only photos and Wikidata
items, both from Wikimedia projects, agree more strongly with each other
(\resRankingBrandsPhotosKendallItems). OSMViews thus sits near the middle of
the other measures: no other function agrees as much with all the rest,
and none duplicates it.

\subsection{Ranking cities}
A city is an area, not a point. Its coordinate in a gazetteer can sit
where few people go, such as the Imperial Palace at the centre of Tokyo,
so for cities we sum each function over a disc of
\resRankingCitiesRadiusKm\,km radius around the coordinate, on pixels about
2.4\,km wide at the equator. We do this for the \resRankingCitiesCities{}
places with at least 15{,}000 inhabitants in GeoNames~\cite{geonames},
leaving out sections of cities, and compare the sums with the population
that GeoNames gives (Table~\ref{tab:cities}).

Only population (\resRankingCitiesPopulationKendallPopulation) and, less
so, night lights (\resRankingCitiesLightsKendallPopulation) follow the size
of a city. OSMViews (\resRankingCitiesOsmviewsKendallPopulation) and the
densities of photos, Wikidata items, places and nature observations hardly
do, yet they agree well with each other: $\tau_b$ between OSMViews and
places is \resRankingCitiesPlacesKendallOsmviews. The most populous cities
are mostly in China, where OpenStreetMap's standard tiles are little used
(\S\ref{sec:coverage}), while the cities with the most views are in Europe,
led by Paris. For cities, then, OSMViews ranks by the attention of map
users, like the other crowd-sourced densities, and not by size; a user who
wants size should rank by population.

\subsection{Wikidata items and QRank}
QRank ranks Wikidata items by their page views across Wikimedia
projects~\cite{qrank}, so it ranks things rather than places. For the
\resQrankItems{} items that have both a coordinate location and a QRank,
the rank correlation between QRank and OSMViews at the item's location is
\resQrankSpearman{} (Spearman), and \resQrankWithViewsPct\,\% of the items
lie on pixels with views. A famous thing can sit in a quiet place and a
little-known one on a busy square, so a weak correlation is expected; the
two signals complement each other.
